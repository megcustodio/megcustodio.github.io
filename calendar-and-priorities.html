When an Executive Says "Yes," Should the Calendar?

On executive accessibility, competing priorities, and the value of protecting time.

Some of the most brilliant executives I've supported have one thing in common: they say "yes" to almost everything.

A quick meeting? Absolutely. Someone needs five minutes? Come on in. A colleague wants to discuss an idea? Let's find some time.

Their intentions are good. They value relationships, want to be accessible, and genuinely believe in an open-door policy.

But there's a cost to being available to everyone, all the time.

I've seen how quickly an executive's calendar can fill with meetings they've agreed to in passing, often without considering what was already scheduled or what else might need their attention.

And sometimes, those commitments never make it onto the calendar at all.

Consider an executive traveling to a conference or visiting customers. While making arrangements, they agree to squeeze in another customer meeting. It makes perfect sense in the moment. They're already in the area, the opportunity is there, and why not make the most of the trip?

Except the person coordinating the travel doesn't know about the additional commitment.

A flight gets changed. Another meeting is scheduled. Suddenly, there's a conflict that could have been avoided with a little communication.

Neither person was necessarily making a bad decision. They were simply working with different information.

You can't protect time you don't know has been committed.

Someone newer to executive support might assume that once the boss says yes, the meeting is a done deal. After all, who are we to question it?

But I believe that's precisely where experience and judgment come into play.

An Executive Business Partner needs to understand not only what's on the calendar, but what the executive is trying to accomplish. What deadlines are approaching? What decisions require uninterrupted thought? Which meetings are genuinely necessary, and which could be handled another way?

And just as importantly, there needs to be enough trust and communication that commitments don't exist in two separate worlds: the executive's and the calendar's.

Sometimes the most valuable thing we can do is ask a simple question:

“I know you agreed to this, but given everything on your plate this week, would you like me to suggest another time?”

That's not overstepping. It's partnership.

The open-door policy doesn't have to disappear. Accessibility is important, and relationships matter. But an open door shouldn't mean an unprotected calendar.

Because when every request becomes a priority, the work that matters most can quietly become the thing that never gets done.

The goal isn't to keep people away from the executive. It's to make sure the executive has the time and space to lead.
